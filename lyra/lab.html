What is new here

Four things her screen does that we could not find elsewhere

The screen follows her hand

When she lifts the omega-3 bottle, the screen changes to “오메가3 · 1캡슐 드세요” before a capsule leaves. The lift itself identifies the item: its container weight left the compartment. There is no camera, no tag and no app. Try “The screen follows her hand”.

One scale tells several items apart

Research prototypes and patents weigh one container per compartment. Lyra puts three bottles on one scale. The weight that left says which item it was, and the weight that came back says how much was used. Look-alikes, such as a 12 g stick and an 11.9 g eye-drop bottle, wait for the evidence instead of guessing.

It reads Korean pharmacy sachets

Each dose time's sachet has its own weight, so Lyra knows a 1.50 g evening sachet from a 1.10 g lunch one. It says “저녁 약봉투예요” when she tears the wrong sachet, and it catches two sachets stuck together. The printed time on the sachet always has the final word.

A day you can read from across the room

The home screen is a 24-hour ring: filled marks for taken doses, a hand for now, and the next dose in the middle. E-ink holds it at 0 W. The rest of the time the screen is quiet and asks only when the scale can't be sure, about 0.7 questions a day in the simulation.

The need is large in Korea. In a national study of older Koreans, 86.4% had polypharmacy (PLOS ONE, 2014). A Korean user-centred design study found that a medication device must fit the pharmacy sachet system. The canister-loading dispensers above are not built for that.
